# A parent's guide to Early Reader

Early Reader helps you teach your own 3- to 5-year-old to read, a few minutes at a time. I built it for my two. It works in English, on a phone or a laptop. Everything you put in it is saved on your device, and there is no server for it to go to.

You run it. The screen tells you what to say and what to show, and keeps track of what each child knows. Your child works with paper cards, letter tiles and a pencil, and with you.

## What you need

- A phone, tablet or laptop with a modern browser.
- A printer, or a cyber café that can print a few pages for you. Paper and a thick marker work too, if you would rather draw the cards yourself.
- A pile of picture books. Library books are fine.
- About 10 minutes a day per child, in short pieces, four or five days a week.

## Putting it on your phone

Open [moshthepitt.github.io/early-reader](https://moshthepitt.github.io/early-reader/) in Chrome. The first visit downloads less than 50 KB. Then open the browser menu (the three dots) and tap "Add to Home screen" or "Install app". On an iPhone, use Share, then "Add to Home Screen".

From then on it opens like any other app, and it works without data or Wi-Fi.

## Getting started

**1. Add your children.** Open the Kids tab and add each child with their first name and birth month. Most 3-year-olds start at stage 1. A 4-year-old who already knows several letter sounds can start at stage 2. You can change the stage at any time.

**2. Print a few things.** On the Print tab, print the letter cards for the letters your child has met (at the very start, print "next 8 letters") and a sheet of letter tiles. When the app checks a letter, cover the picture on the card with your thumb, or use the cards without pictures. Card lasts longer than paper. Cut along the dotted lines. No printer? Save the page as a PDF from the print screen and take it to a cyber, or copy the letters onto cut-up cereal boxes with a marker.

**3. Learn to say the sounds.** This part is for you, and it matters more than it looks. Letters have names ("em") and sounds ("mmm"). Reading runs on the sounds, said short and clean. Many of us add an "uh" without noticing - "muh" instead of "mmm", "tuh" instead of a quick "t" - and that "uh" makes blending much harder for a child. The Sounds tab shows how to say each one. Record yourself and listen back. It's a bit embarrassing. Do it anyway.

## Every day: two kinds of time

### Story time

One or two books a day, with both children together if you have two. This is where vocabulary, talk and a love of books come from. It matters as much as the letters do, and the studies say its benefits last longer.

Reading the book to them is the start. The bigger win is getting them to talk. Ask a question, let them answer, repeat what they said and add a little ("Yes, a dog! A big, muddy dog."), then ask again so they can use the new words. Three to five questions per book is plenty. Keep the story moving.

A few kinds of question that work:

- Leave a gap at the end of a line for them to fill in: "The cat sat on the ___."
- Ask what happened: "What did the bear do at the start?"
- Ask about the picture: "Tell me what's happening here."
- Ask what, where and why: "Why is she sad?"
- Link it to their life: "Have you been to the beach like this?"

Point at the words now and then. Young children look at the pictures and almost never at the print unless you show them. Run your finger under a line as you read it, or ask, "Can you find a letter from your name on this page?"

The Story time tab has more prompts, and it can prepare questions for a specific book (see "Using AI" below).

### Practice bursts

Two to four short bursts a day for each child, two or three minutes each, with gaps in between - after breakfast, after a nap, before the bath. In the studies, several short goes on different days did better than one long lesson.

On the Today tab, tap "Start a 2-minute burst". Your child picks the card game or the tile game. Then, for each step:

1. Read the script and do what it says.
2. Tap **Right first try** or **Needed help**. Only the first try counts. A pause to think is fine: if their first answer was right and you didn't help, it's right first try.
3. After a miss, the app shows you what to say. Show them, let them say it with you, and move on.

The app decides what comes up: letters due for review, at most one new thing, and an easy win at the end. A letter counts as known once your child gets it right first try on two different days. On a day they needed help with it, a right answer later that day is practice; the app checks again tomorrow.

Keep each child's bursts separate. A younger sibling listening in is fine; they often pick things up that way.

## When it gets hard

Stop when they're upset. Tears, refusal, or two misses in a row mean it's time to finish on something easy. The Stop button does exactly that. Pick it up again later or tomorrow.

A break is fine. Weeks, even months. When you come back, the app starts with review, and letters usually return quickly.

Aim for about 8 right out of 10. The Progress tab shows this for the week. Much lower than that and the work is too hard: slow down. Always perfect and you can add letters faster (Settings, "new letters per week").

Praise the work, not the child. "You said each sound and slid them together" helps more than "You're so clever". The scripts suggest lines like this after each right answer.

Expect a stall at blending. Going from knowing letter sounds to reading "s-a-t... sat" is the step where most children get stuck, sometimes for months. Many 4-year-olds aren't ready yet. That is normal and not a sign of anything wrong.

Don't mistake memory for reading. Children are good at memorising favourite books and familiar words. At stage 3 the app slips in made-up "monster names" like "fip" and words they have never seen. Those show whether they're really sounding out. Don't help on those.

## The four stages

**Stage 1: sound play** (often age 3). Listening games with no letters: rhyming, clapping the beats in words, spotting which picture starts with a different sound, and "robot talk" ("c-at... what did I say?"). Plus the letters in their own name. Moves on when they can hear first sounds reliably over several days and know the first letter of their name.

**Stage 2: letter sounds** (late 3 to 4). Letter names and sounds together, two to four new letters a week. Each is taught with a picture; checks show the letter alone. Moves on at about 12 known letter sounds, including at least two vowels (a, e, i, o, u).

**Stage 3: blending** (around 4, often later). Sounding out short words like "sat", "mat" and "fun", holding each sound into the next: "sssaaat". Building words with tiles. Moves on when they can sound out words they have never seen, most of the time, on several days.

**Stage 4: first reading.** Tricky words that don't sound like their letters ("the", "was", "said"), two-letter sounds (sh, ch, th, ee, oo) and short sentences and stories. The Texts tab lists the ones your child can read right now, and the Print tab turns them into little booklets they can illustrate.

The app suggests when a child looks ready to move up. You decide.

## Using AI (optional)

You don't need AI to use Early Reader. Two features use it, and in both you stay in charge:

- **Questions for a book.** On the Story time tab, type a book's title (and, if you like, a note on what happens on each page). The app writes a prompt you copy into whatever AI chat tool you use. Read what comes back before using it - AI sometimes invents details about books it doesn't know.
- **New practice stories.** On the Texts tab, the app writes a prompt that lists the only words your child can read. Paste the story you get back into "Check a text". Every word is checked against what your child has been taught, and AI breaks these rules often, so swap out anything marked red.

The app removes your children's names from these prompts. Don't type other personal details into them.

One firm rule: AI is for you, not your child. Common Sense Media advises no AI chatbots for children aged five and under. There is no chat feature in this app, on purpose.

## Your data

Everything you put into Early Reader is saved on your device, in your browser: your children's names, their progress and any sounds you record. There is no Early Reader server and no account, so there is nowhere for any of it to be sent. [How it works](how-it-works.md) explains this in full.

The catch: if you clear your browser data or lose the phone, it's gone. Use Settings, "Export backup" now and then. A backup file restores everything, recordings included.

## What to expect

Teaching letters and sounds early gives real skills in the short term. It does not give a lasting head start on its own: children who start reading later tend to catch up by about age 11. The things that seem to last are vocabulary, a love of books, and getting to reading without it becoming a battle. In English, getting to solid sounding-out takes about two and a half years, so this is a slow road.

My own measure is simple. Can they sound out a word they have never seen? Do they talk about the stories? Do they still want to do it tomorrow?

## Where this comes from

Every choice in the app is based on research on how young children learn to read, what parents have tried at home, and what AI can and can't do at this age.
